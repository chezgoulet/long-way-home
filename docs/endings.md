# Endings: arrival, readers, and the joke

The owner's design of 2026-10-06. The voyage can end, and what happens at the end is not a finale — it is a
**reading**. This document records the shape, the destinations, and the one law that keeps multiple endings
from eating the north star.

## The joke ending

**The game can end in arrival at Earth, and it will be a joke ending.** Nothing is scripted for arrival. At
the end of seventy-odd years of game time, the player gets **three seconds of Voyager coming out of warp into
a system** — which is the entire arrival the series finale ever showed us.

Why this is right, in terms a future builder will still understand, because it is exactly the kind of decision
that gets silently undone:

- **Scripting an arrival would repeat the show's own error.** It would spend a beat that was never earned.
  The finale handed us four seconds because seven years had not accrued into the moment. A run of *this* game
  accrues for seventy years; a parade at the end would be the same mistake, taken more slowly.
- **The refusal is faithful, not contemptuous.** It lands on the show, not on the player — provided the game
  never promised a parade and the crew stopped promising each other one.
- **The asset rule has already made the clip for us.** No game assets enter the repository, so the three
  seconds cannot be Paramount's footage: it must be our own engine doing what it already does, warp-in against
  a skybox, filmed from our own camera. Which is very nearly the shot the show gave us, and is legally forced
  to be ours. The constraint and the artistic choice agree.
- **The formal joke, worth naming rather than discovering by accident:** those three seconds are the only
  three seconds in the entire game in which the player has no decisions.

**Write it down as a subtraction.** It belongs in the creed's list of deliberate refusals, beside no point
defence and no time travel, with the reason attached. That list is the only thing standing between this
decision and an agent two years from now deciding the ending looks unfinished and fixing it.

## The last frame is the log

The clip is punctuation, not the ending. The player arrives, the counter stops, and the game hands back the
run: the whole record, searchable, with the sealed decks, the empty posts, the names, the reports they signed
and the ones they edited (`docs/the-record-and-the-log.md`).

It is the only ending this design can have that another game cannot copy, because no other game kept the
record. **Arrival is where the counter stops; the log is where the run ends** — and the player is the only one
who can read it properly.

## Two arrivals, one clip

- **The grind.** Seventy years on the long approach, the crew getting louder as home comes into range,
  Federation contact returning, Klingons in the shipping lanes again.
- **The conduit.** Out of a transwarp aperture in three seconds, with no approach at all.

Same clip. Whether the run took seventy years or seventy seconds, the game gives it three seconds. **The clip
is the constant; the route is the variable.** Choosing Qo'noS and still receiving the finale's shot is funnier
than choosing Earth and receiving it.

## Destinations are readers

The endgame's central decision is not *how do we get home*. It is **whose record we become** — which is the
fourth of the atlas's four moral questions, *when do we turn away*, finally pointed inward at the player
instead of at a stranger.

**A destination declares which part of the record it reads.** Starfleet reads the month reports and the
regulations. The Klingons read the deaths. A settlement reads whether you kept your word to them three years
ago. One document, different charge sheets — so a destination is *a lens plus a place*, and authoring one is a
data exercise rather than an ending-sized programme of work.

**The set is accrued, not selected.** `docs/exploration-and-science.md` already says reputation travels ahead
of the ship and the Delta Quadrant is a small place, so who will take you is a function of the run you kept.
The ending has breeding conditions like everything else here. You do not get to decide on the last day to hand
yourselves to somebody; they have to want you, or at least not fire on you. **The available set is earned; the
choice among them is free.**

## The law: readers, not verdicts

**No destination is better than another.** Endings are readings of a life, not rankings of it.

The design already says there is **exactly one unwinnable end state, the core breach**
(`docs/failure-is-content.md`), so everything else has to be a different reading rather than a better or worse
outcome. If one ending is *the good ending*, then seventy years of decisions collapse into optimising toward
it, the player reloads at the last decision, and the reset button comes back in through the endgame door.

## Earth

**Always reachable; not always welcoming.** You can always sail home — the reading may be a trial. That keeps
the joke ending permanently available and gives the fastest route the worst audit: come home through the
conduit, by theft, and the report is a mess. Canon does exactly this.

Earth and Starfleet are the reader who judges by regulation, and the only reader that can **promote** rather
than merely accept.

**Arriving at Earth and reporting to Starfleet are separable.** That gives the game one last question, and it
is the Sisko question at full scale: *we are home, and do we file.* Whichever way it is answered, the game
then cuts to the log.

## The Klingon Empire, for asylum

The owner's destination for a run whose record would not survive a Starfleet inquiry. The hope is that the
Klingons respect the crew's warrior nature and grant asylum from the Federation, even at the cost of making
diplomacy a little difficult.

**Canon forecloses the obvious alternative.** By 2378 the Maquis are finished: the Dominion and the
Cardassians destroyed the movement in 2373 while Voyager was on the far side of the galaxy, so there is no
territory, no government and no power to grant anybody anything. **The Maquis are not a port.** But the
consequence is better than the destination was:

- **Their allegiance cannot resolve outward at all, only inward.** Every Maquis-weighted crew member at the
  end of a run is a person whose cause lost, whose comrades are dead, and who has worn a Starfleet uniform
  under protest for seven years.
- **The Klingon option is the only answer in the game to the question they have been carrying**, because it is
  the only place that would say *you are a warrior* rather than *you are a provisional officer*. The Klingon
  ending is the Maquis ending, with the Maquis having nowhere to go.
- **It makes a line already in the corpus load-bearing.** `docs/crew-manifest.md` records that a
  field-commissioned Maquis officer holds a *provisional* rank. To Starfleet that is a courtesy; to the
  Klingons it is a fact about whether the oath was ever real.

**The lens is honour, not violence.** Klingons revere honour, so the Klingon read is not *you fought well*.
It is: did you stand when it cost you, did you keep your word, did you bury your dead properly, and did you
tell the truth about it. The contrast with Starfleet is exact — **Starfleet reads the paperwork; the Klingons
read the deaths.** A run with a clean log and few battles fails the Klingon read; a run with a butchered log
and many battles fails the Starfleet read. The player can see, most of the way through, which one they have
been building for.

**It is the one destination whose reading reaches outside the ship.** The Federation and the Empire are allies
by then, so an asylum grant is a crack in an alliance. That is legitimate under the rule that the world
*reads* the record rather than being edited by the player — and it should stay unique to this destination,
because if every ending changed the galaxy, none of them would.

## The other destinations, briefly

- **A Delta Quadrant home.** The ones who stay, scaled up: the whole crew landing rather than individuals
  leaving (`docs/scenario-atlas.md`, scenario 4). Canon's landing mechanism is sourced in our own research —
  the Intrepid class can enter an atmosphere and land, with antigravity generators and lifters, and the crew
  go to blue alert for a landing (`docs/research/raw/Intrepid_class.txt`, citing *The 37's*). **The
  settlement offer in that episode should be confirmed against the episode before authoring it**, per the
  canon discipline in `docs/lore-ledger.md`.
- **A half-assimilated arrival.** The reader may be the Collective, or a liberated Unimatrix; the run ends as
  a horror rather than a homecoming.
- **The cynical one.** Sell the ship.
- **No reader at all.** Nobody reads the log because there is nobody left who wants to. This is where
  barbarism finally lands — and it reads in the log as **the log going quiet**: reports stop being false and
  start being absent, because nobody is writing them any more.

## Transwarp

The corpus already names this ending once, and in an unexpected place: `docs/crew-manifest.md` records that
the Hazard Team's arc in the game's own lore is defeating the Vohrsoth and getting Voyager home **through the
transwarp hub**, and calls it the journey's endgame. It has never been designed.

- **It is the antithesis of the north star, which is why it belongs.** The design says the shortcut costs a
  principle, a crew member, or a year. Transwarp costs **all three**, and it is the only route that collapses
  seventy years into a single event. Everything else is spending small amounts over a long time; this is one
  enormous bet with the whole run on the table.
- **It is canon's own finale**, so the conduit run's arrival literally is the series finale's arrival. We can
  honour the finale both ways: by grinding to it, or by arriving the way the show did and getting the same
  three seconds for it.
- **It requires the enemy's knowledge.** A hub is a Borg structure, a coil comes off a Borg ship, the conduit
  is the Collective's road. So the shortcut cannot be taken unless Borg knowledge is aboard — Seven, a
  recovered drone, a Borg-recovered allegiance, a section of the ship never got back. **The playstyle of a
  long war for a hull that assimilation has most of is also the run most likely to reach the conduit.** Horror
  and hope out of the same fact, and worth protecting.
- **Sequencing.** Transwarp is Borg, and the owner has deferred the Borg universe work until the mechanics are
  stable. That deferral is safe — nothing in the current design assumes a Borg story, the model is pressure
  per compartment with configurable controller and compromise, and strategic awareness is a named gap rather
  than an assumed system. But the conduit playthrough is the one ending whose content is gated behind that
  decision.

## The last stretch

**The counter is the instrument for it.** The estimate is a projection from current capability and the
derivative since the last entry is the story. For most of the run that arrow points the wrong way in small
amounts. Coming home it turns and turns hard — decades collapsing to years to months — and that single number
does more emotional work than any content we could author. The crew's excitement should be visible there
before it is visible anywhere else.

**The Alpha Quadrant is not safety; it is jurisdiction.** For seventy years the player has been the highest
authority in the universe: nobody out there could overrule them or read their books. Crossing back means the
record becomes a document with an audience that can punish, and the fudged reports, the written-off decks and
the spent people stop being private. That is the veracity mechanic's final exam
(`docs/the-record-and-the-log.md`) and the *eleven minutes* scenario at full scale
(`docs/scenario-atlas.md`, scenario 6). It gives the last stretch a register unlike anything before it: not
*can we hold the ship*, but **will this stand up**.

**And some of the crew do not want to arrive.** They have built a life, a post and a person, and Earth is
where that ends rather than where it starts. Free beat, straight out of canon.

## Structure

- **The available destinations are accrued** from reputation, and at least one is always open — Earth, because
  you can always sail home. So the choice never becomes *the game chose for me*.
- **The crew get a vote.** The destination is the largest possible use of the soft authority gate, and it is
  the natural home of the relief-of-command failure state `docs/morale.md` names. **The final expression of
  the captain's failure mode — losing the crew's confidence — is the run where they choose the destination
  instead of you.** Recommended: the veto fires once, at the destination, and nowhere earlier.
- **A wildcard is attemptable.** The player can make a play for somewhere that has not invited them, and fail
  to reach it or be refused at it. Recommended, because the wildcard is the one place in the endgame where
  something is spent that cannot be got back, on a hope.

## Budget

An ending is a **reading with a place attached**, and the reading is where the content goes. Most destinations
are a lens plus a short scene; only the richest two — Earth and Qo'noS — justify a built location, and the
joke ending says Earth's arrival gets three seconds regardless. **The endings are cheap by design; the money
goes into the reading.**

## Acceptance

- Arrival plays three seconds of our own engine's warp-in, with no scripted content, and the run then hands
  the player the log as its last frame.
- The arrival clip is identical for every destination and for every route, including the conduit.
- Each destination declares which part of the record it reads, and two destinations reading the same run
  reach different, attributable conclusions.
- The available destination set is derived from reputation, and Earth is always in it.
- No destination is presented as a better or worse outcome than another, in text, in UI, or in the log.
- The crew's veto can fire once at the destination, and the log records who holds the conn after it does.
- A wildcard attempt can fail, and the failure is attributable to what the player did.
- The refusal to script an arrival is recorded in the creed's subtraction list, with its reason.
- And the human check: a player at the end should be able to say **what they chose to lose**, and who was
  entitled to read it.
