# Multiplayer premise: the chair is empty

The owner's beat, 2026-10-05: **in multiplayer, the command crew was killed when Voyager was brought to the
Delta Quadrant by the Caretaker's array, and getting home falls to the rest of the crew.** This is the
premise for mode 3's multiplayer. It is recorded here with what it buys, what it costs, and the canon it
stands on.

## What it buys

1. **It is the single-player premise, told from the other side.** The programme's emotional engine is that
   *your rank rises because people died* -- posts empty, the roster promotes, someone untrained ends up in a
   job they did not ask for. In single player the player works under a captain. In multiplayer the captain is
   one of the players, because there is no captain. Same machine, different occupant.
2. **It solves the extra-captain problem cleanly.** You cannot have four human players on a bridge that a
   canon character is also commanding. An empty chair removes the conflict rather than working around it.
3. **It justifies field commissions, which canon already normalises.** A Maquis made chief engineer, a young
   ensign left on the bridge, a civilian given a department -- that is Voyager's own history. A crew of
   survivors holding a ship together is not a deviation from Star Trek; it is the show's most Star Trek
   material.
4. **It makes authority the first question of the game.** With the senior staff gone, *who holds the conn* is
   not established by the writers -- it is established by the players, and it can change hands through injury,
   death, agreement or mutiny. That is the drama the one-ship-one-server design was built for, and it now has
   a reason to exist from the first minute.
5. **It teaches the tone in the first thirty seconds.** The first thing the game tells you is that the ship's
   leadership is already gone and nobody is coming. No reset button, understood immediately.

## What it costs, and what to protect

- **It spends the canon cast**, who are the most evocative assets the game owns. So be deliberate about who
  survives: **the Doctor** is a hologram and cannot be killed by a displacement wave, and canon gives him a
  whole arc about becoming a person; **Neelix and Kes** are Delta Quadrant locals, which makes them the crew's
  only guide and the premise's most useful survivors; the Hazard Team is already ours. **Seven of Nine** is a
  2374 arrival, which means the cast can *grow* -- the game does not have to start with everyone it will have.
- **A ship with no officers is less Starfleet.** The improvisation should read as *the crew trying to remain
  Starfleet without anyone to tell them how*, not as a crew who stopped caring. That tension is the point.
- **The Maquis question becomes the players' problem.** Canon merges two crews under a captain who imposes it.
  Here, nobody imposes it: the integration is a decision the players have to make and live with, and the
  resentment on both sides is real. That is a much better multiplayer story than a briefing.

## The canon it stands on

Voyager's crossing cost **twelve or more dead**, including the first officer, the chief engineer, the entire
medical staff and the transporter chief; her warp core took microfractures from the displacement wave; and the
Kazon took the array, which is why nobody came back for them. All of that is canon and sourced in
`docs/research/voyager-interior-systems.md` and `docs/research/voyager-damage-borg.md`.

**The extension is ours, and must be labelled as ours:** the casualty list reaching all the way to the
command crew. Canon's senior staff survived; multiplayer's does not. That is a deliberate branch, not a
retelling.

## Decision: multiplayer is its own branch (2026-10-05, owner)

Confirmed. The Caretaker events cost the command crew **in multiplayer's telling**, and multiplayer is its own
branch of the story. Single player keeps canon's cast, and the retail campaign stays untouched canon.

Multiplayer therefore starts with the chair empty and the players deciding who sits in it, while single player
starts with a captain and a chain of command -- and, per `docs/start-states.md`, may optionally start with any
number of those command posts already vacant, if that is the game the player wants to play.

## What it implies mechanically

- **Authority becomes a field on the crew record**: who holds the conn, who can authorise an ejection, who may
  order a deck sealed. The rank-and-role design already assumes this; the premise just sets its initial state
  to *empty at the top*.
- **The first log entry is the premise**: twelve dead, senior staff among them, ship damaged, no rescue
  coming. The ship's own record carries it, so every later entry is measured against it.
- **Command can change hands mid-run**, which means it needs rules: seniority by rank, then department, then
  the crew's own agreement -- and a way to resolve a contested chair that is not simply a menu.

## Acceptance

- The premise appears in the ship's own log, and the roster starts with the senior posts vacant.
- Players can hold, contest and lose the conn, and the ship records who holds it.
- The canon cast's fate is stated explicitly, and the difference from canon is labelled as our branch.
- And the human check: the first session should feel like inheriting a ship, not like being handed one.
